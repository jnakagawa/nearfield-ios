A tone is a wave

A sound is just air moving out and back, fast and steady. Drawn along time, it's a wave — rising and falling over and over.

Two tones, two waves

Play two tones at once and you have two waves running side by side. Right now they're the same, moving together.

They add up

Your ear hears the two waves added together. Where two peaks meet, the sound is louder. Where a peak lands on a dip, they cancel.

Keep scrolling — watch them slide from lined-up (loud) to opposite (silent) and back.

The beat

Now make the two tones a little different. The lineup keeps drifting on its own — so the combined wave swells and fades, over and over.

That slow swell-and-fade is the beat.

In tune, out of tune

Bring the two tones together and the beat slows — right as they match, it stops. Perfectly smooth. That's in tune.

Pull them apart and it speeds into a buzz, until your ear hears two separate notes again.

In the piece, two people coming close is two tones sliding toward that still, in-tune point.

The layers

Nearfield stacks a few kinds of tone together — high and low, fast and slow — the way a gamelan is built:

  • the pulse — low and slow, marking time · the gongs
  • the bones — the tone at the center · balungan
  • the ornament — high and fine, only when you move · the elaboration
  • the conductor — silent, keeping the cycle · kendhang

Every phone is one voice in one of these layers.

One field

A whole room of these waves, crossing in the air — loud where they agree, quiet where they cancel. The music happens between the phones.

Your screen shows your corner of it. Together, you are the field.
