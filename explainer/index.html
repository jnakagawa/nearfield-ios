NEARFIELD

A PIECE FOR MANY PHONES

Every phone plays one tone. Together they make one field of sound. Here's how it works.

SCROLL ↓

Two waves

Every tone is a wave — the air pushing and pulling, fast. Play two tones at once and the waves add together: where two pushes line up it gets louder, where a push meets a pull they cancel.

If the two tones are a little different, that lineup keeps drifting — in, out, in, out — so the sound swells and fades. That swell-and-fade is the beat.

the pulse: —

In tune, out of tune

Bring the two tones together and the drift slows — right as they match, it stops. Perfectly smooth. That's what in tune means.

Pull them apart and the pulse speeds up, into a buzz, until your ear hears two separate notes again.

In the piece, two people coming close is two tones sliding toward that still, in-tune point.

The layers

Nearfield isn't a melody with backing. It's a few kinds of tone stacked together — the way a gamelan is built.

  • the pulse — low and slow, marking time · the gongs
  • the bones — the tone at the center of it all · balungan
  • the ornament — high and fine, only when you move · the elaboration
  • the conductor — silent, keeping the cycle · kendhang

Every phone is one voice in one of these layers. The music is where they overlap.

The cycle

The piece moves in cycles inside cycles, each closed by a low gong. Between them it breathes — thickening with fine detail, then thinning back out.

Over sixteen minutes it swells from a bare opening to a dense, shimmering middle, and settles again.

One point

At the last gong, every wave in the room lines up on the same point — hundreds of phones, one field.

Your screen shows your corner of it. Together, you are the field.
